# Canadian CNC Machining Industry — Market Context

## Ontario Manufacturing, Overall

- Ontario's manufacturing sector supports **more than 787,000 jobs**
- Contributes **11% of provincial GDP**
- Accounts for nearly **80% of merchandise exports**
- Manufacturing employment grew **2.7% year-over-year** in 2024

*(Source: Government of Canada, Job Bank sectoral reporting)*

## Machinery Manufacturing (NAICS 333), Ontario — 2023

- **Employment:** 58,400 workers — 40.6% of Canada's total machinery-manufacturing workforce
- **GDP contribution:** 9.5% of Ontario's manufacturing GDP, up 6.0% from the prior year
- **Exports:** ~8.3% of Ontario's total exports; over 81% destined for the United States
- **Average hourly wage:** $30.45 (2023) — just under the manufacturing-sector average ($30.76), above the provincial average ($29.18)
- **Workforce profile:** 80% male (vs. 50% provincial average); 31.2% aged 55+; 96.7% full-time

*(Source: Government of Canada, Job Bank — direct fetch, jobbank.gc.ca)*

## CNC Machinist Wages (Ontario)

- Range: $18.75–$39.00/hour; median $28.00/hour
- Annual range: roughly $45,000–$65,000

## Industry Scale (National, Directional)

Industry-association and trade-press estimates (not government-verified) put Canada's CNC machining sector at roughly **3,888 machine shops** producing **$7 billion in annual revenue**, concentrated in Ontario and Quebec. Treat this figure as directional context, not an audited statistic — it comes from trade publications rather than Statistics Canada.

## Outlook

Job Bank's 2024–2026 outlook for Ontario machinery manufacturing describes "modest but steady growth," driven by non-residential construction investment, automotive capacity expansion, nuclear refurbishment projects, and continued automation adoption — offset by an aging workforce and persistent skill shortages.

## Why this matters for a capacity-exchange marketplace

This is the demand-side backdrop: a large (58,400-worker), geographically concentrated (Toronto / Kitchener-Waterloo / Windsor-Sarnia), aging (31% over 55) machinist workforce, growing modestly, with heavy export exposure to the US — exactly the conditions the "idle second shift" thesis depends on: real capacity, real skill scarcity, and thin, fragmented information about who has spare time on which machine.
